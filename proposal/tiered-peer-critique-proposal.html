A Tiered Peer-Critique Architecture for AI Answers

Every major lab's models sit on a panel and argue it out. A hub that never writes asks the questions.

George W. Lott

Working draft v1.3, October 2026, with results of a second pilot. This version: DOI pending. Supersedes v1.2, 10.5281/zenodo.22357227, and v1.1, 10.5281/zenodo.22325883.

Summary

No single AI model is best at everything, and none is reliable at knowing its own weak spots. Convening a panel is now a shipped feature. This proposal is about what happens after the panel is convened: the answer has to earn its release.

Every request runs through the current publicly available models from every major lab, always the latest live version of each, sorted into class tiers. The models break the request down, bid on its parts, challenge each other's drafts, and revise until they agree. A dedicated hub that never writes an answer questions the result until nothing is left open, releases it with a note on where the panel split, and keeps a running catalogue of who was challenged, who was corrected, and who was later proven wrong, so the same class of mistake is caught sooner next time.

Where an answer can be checked, this should beat the best single model by a measurable margin. Where it can't, the design says so rather than pretending otherwise. Two pilots that could show either result are specified at the end, and their results are reported there. The first, on 64 checkable tasks, could not separate the conditions on correctness because every answered item was right. The second, on 104 harder tasks, found a mid-priced panel with a frontier hub 4.8 points below one pass of the frontier model and 1.0 point below the same panel without the hub, both intervals including zero; the hub's net effect on right answers was zero in one run and minus four in the next, and the model that served as the hub, writing alone, outscored the panel it interrogated.

Debate between models, councils of models, and judges of models all exist; the prior-work section says where. These six elements are what the design contributes, each named against its nearest neighbor.

The panel

Membership. The major labs' hosted models by default, always the latest live version of each. Open-weight models (Llama, Mistral, DeepSeek, Qwen and the like) are available as an add-on.

Tiers. Models are grouped by class, and each tier pulls the equivalent model from every lab. The tier a user pays for decides which classes work the request. Every tier runs the same process; cheaper tiers simply run it with cheaper models.

Light Haiku-class and equivalents. Everyday use at the lowest cost. Agreement within the tier
Mid Sonnet-class and equivalents. Everyday use with more headroom. Agreement within the tier
Frontier Opus/Fable-class and equivalents. High-stakes work. Agreement within the tier
Apex tier Every class at once. For those who can pay for it. Agreement across tiers, weighted toward the frontier

At the Apex tier, the frontier models choose the final answer by looking at where the tiers agree. That agreement is weighted rather than counted one model, one vote: a lab's light model is usually distilled from its frontier model, so the two tend to be wrong in the same places, and agreement between them adds little. The lighter tiers earn their seat a different way, as cheap coverage. They catch omissions, offer alternative readings, and give a fast first pass. When a light model disagrees with a frontier model, it is more often the light model that is wrong, and the split is still recorded.

In a perfect world every request would run at the Apex tier. The tiers exist because that isn't affordable for the everyday user today.

The hub

A dedicated model that never sits on the panel, so it has no answer of its own to defend. It does not choose a model, edit the answer, or define it. It asks.

What it asks

What is the source. Why did seat B disagree. What happens if X. It keeps asking until nothing is left open, within a question budget, so interrogation neither loops forever nor turns into a rubber stamp.

What it must be

At least the class of the highest tier in play. A questioner weaker than the panel it examines gets talked past by confident wrong answers, which is what the weak-judge experiments found once the capability gap widens [4, 5]. Two things narrow that gap: the hub can enforce checks it doesn't need to understand (run the code, fetch the source, recompute the figure), the way a clerk enforces procedure, and the catalogue hands it patterned questions drawn from past failures.

It also needs a rule for the case where it cannot answer. In the second pilot's second pass the hub gave up on 4 of 104 tasks, once on a content filter and three times at its reply cap; a hub that does not answer can neither block release nor grant it, so the answer ships flagged, as a deadlock does. And its budget is a setting that shapes the result: at six questions the hub used all six on three quarters of the tasks, so the release conditions never stopped it; a hub that asks until its budget is gone is adding rounds, not interrogating.

Where it comes from

Every lab has a model on the panel, so the hub is either supplied by a rotating lab or trained for the role. Either way, it keeps the catalogue.

How a request moves

The same six steps run in every tier.

  1. Decomposition by the panelThe panel proposes a breakdown of the request into components and critiques it the same way it critiques answers. At the Apex tier the frontier models propose the breakdown and the lighter tiers work from it, so the request isn't debated twice. The hub does not decompose.
  2. Bidding, not assignmentEach model puts up which components it is prepared to answer. Bids are equal: no central picker, no ranking by reputation. Models can hand components to each other mid-stream, and one model may claim the whole request.
  3. Mandatory critiqueEvery draft, whole or partial, is challenged by the other models before it goes anywhere. Drafts are attributed by seat, not by name, so a challenge targets the content rather than the label on it, and every challenge has to point at evidence. Critique is what separates good bids from bad ones.
  4. Iterative moldingThe answer is revised across rounds, by different models, until the panel agrees it is ready to submit. Each tier has a round budget.
  5. InterrogationThe hub questions the submitted answer until it has nothing left to ask.
  6. ReleaseThe user receives the answer, a note on where the panel split, and the full transcript on demand.

Release conditions

All three are required before an answer leaves the system.

  • The panel agrees: within the tier on single-tier plans, across tiers at the Apex tier.
  • The hub has no open questions left.
  • Every verifiable check passes: code runs, sources are found, numbers recompute.

Deadlock. If the panel can't agree, or a hub question can't be answered because no source exists, within the round or time limit, lower tiers may escalate to the next tier up where the plan allows. At the Apex tier there is nothing above, so the best draft ships with the dispute flagged to the user.

A seat that declines a step after winning its bid abstains, and an abstention is not an objection; its draft, revision or reply passes to another seat. A hub that declines leaves the answer flagged. Nothing in the protocol lets one participant's silence end the request. And no revision or correction replaces the standing answer unless it carries the answer format the request asks for; a text that has lost it is refused and the standing answer kept, with the refusal recorded.

Guarding against shared blind spots

Because the models trained on much the same data, unanimous agreement can be unanimous error [11, 12].

Version 1

Cross-tier agreement plus the verifiable checks.

The fuller version

A designated dissenter each round, whose only job is to attack the consensus, and a rule that no factual claim passes without a source or a check attached.

The catalogue

The hub records who bid on what, who was challenged on what, where the panel split, what was corrected, and what was later found wrong. It uses that record to spot the same class of inaccuracy sooner and to sharpen its own questions. As implemented in the pilot harness the catalogue is a per-seat digest of counts and recent challenge snippets, not a record of specific failures; the second pilot tested that digest and nothing more.

Two layers
A global record of patterns across all users, and a private per-user layer.
Ground truth
The catalogue logs an error, rather than a disagreement, only once something confirms who was right: user feedback and corrections, automated checks, and outcomes that surface later (a prediction, a figure, or a diagnosis confirmed down the line). All three feed the record.
Version stamps
Because the panel always runs the latest live version of each model, every entry is stamped with the model version it describes. A pattern that belonged to last quarter's model is evidence about this quarter's, not a verdict.
Retention
Transcripts on demand plus a per-user layer means storing a great deal. The retention policy is a day-one decision, not a later one.

Where the gain lives

Where an answer can be checked (math, code, lookups, data work), the panel should beat the best single model by a measurable margin, and the verifiable-checks rule is what makes the gain stick. The second pilot did not find that margin at the mid tier on checkable tasks, and its checks, written by the hub, failed on right answers seven times out of ten. The design's claim now rests on the frontier tier, which has not been run, and on a check rule that verifies its own expressions. On open-ended judgment, writing, and taste, the evidence points the other way: consensus flattens answers, and models tend to cave under critique even when they were right. There, the panel's honest output is the split note, not a better paragraph.

The cost is real. A panel of N models over R rounds spends tens of times the tokens of a single answer and takes minutes rather than seconds. That is acceptable at the Apex tier and needs a strict round budget at the light tier.

One benchmark rule applies before anything is priced: each tier is measured against a single frontier-class model, not against a single model of its own class. A panel of light-class models will not generally beat one frontier model, and a tier that can't should be priced and described accordingly.

Prior work

This design sits inside two lines of research and borrows from a third. Naming them is meant to show where the proposal starts, not to claim any of it.

Debate as oversight

Irving, Christiano and Amodei proposed in 2018 that two AIs argue before a judge, on the reasoning that a lie is harder to defend than to refute [1]. Experiments with human judges followed [2], then with model judges: Khan and colleagues found that more persuasive debaters make judges more accurate [3], and Kenton and colleagues put weak model judges over strong debaters across extractive QA, math and code, finding that debate beats a single consultant, that its edge over the judge answering alone is mixed once there is no information asymmetry, and that stronger debaters help only modestly [4]. Later work has modeled how oversight scales with the capability gap [5] and studied collaborative rather than adversarial resolution of disagreements [6]. The hub here is a descendant of the judge with one role removed: it never decides for a side and never writes an answer.

Multi-agent debate for accuracy

Du and colleagues showed in 2023 that several instances of a model answering, reading each other, and revising over fixed rounds improves math and factual accuracy [7]. Variants added personas and assigned roles [8], and mixture-of-agents arranged models from different labs in layers with an aggregator writing the final answer [9]. Recent work goes after the failure modes: debate hacking, where debaters mislead the judge with overconfident claims and end up below single-agent baselines, answered with a non-zero-sum protocol [10]; homogeneous reasoning paths and deadlock [11]; the roles of confidence and diversity [12]; and authority bias in multi-agent evaluation [13]. The panel here is the collaborative, cross-lab branch of this line, with the aggregator removed.

Judges, routers and aggregators

Using a model as a judge is standard practice and has known biases, including a preference for a model's own outputs [14, 15]. Routers such as RouteLLM learn to send each request to one model [16], and commercial gateways do the same in production. Bidding for work goes back to the contract net of 1980 [17]. This design uses none of them to pick a model: bidding replaces routing, critique replaces the aggregator, and a questioner replaces the judge.

What the field has found wrong with all of this

  • A single model with a strong prompt matches the best discussion methods on a wide range of reasoning tasks [18]; debate strategies do not reliably beat self-consistency and ensembling once cost is counted [19]; and under equal thinking-token budgets, single agents have been reported to beat multi-agent systems on multi-hop reasoning [22]. This is why the pilot below is compute-matched: the panel is compared against one frontier model given the same token budget, not against one pass.
  • Judges get talked past [4, 10], defer to authority [13], and are vulnerable to persuasive but wrong reasoning [20, 23]. This is why critique is seat-anonymous and evidence-required, why a dissenter is designated, and why the hub enforces checks it does not need to understand.
  • Models trained on the same data share blind spots, so agreement is weak evidence [11, 12]. This is why agreement is weighted across labs and tiers rather than counted, and why nothing ships without a verifiable check where one exists.

Three recent results point the same way as the design. The one lever that made debate competitive in Smit and colleagues' benchmark was tuning how readily agents agree [19], which is the quantity the release gate and the designated dissenter control directly. A 2026 study of debate for data cleaning reports that self-verification with identical tools failed, and that a separate critic grounded in code execution with evidence-gated generation was the first debate configuration to significantly beat a single agent on a generative task [24]: a separate questioner, mechanical checks, and an evidence rule are three of this design's six elements. And heterogeneous panels, models from different labs rather than copies of one, have been reported to show honest gains and resilience under adversarial peers [25], which is the case for filling every seat from a different lab.

What this proposal does not claim

  • That debate, councils, or model judges are new. They are not.
  • That the panel beats a single model on open-ended work. The evidence says it flattens such answers, and the design's output there is the split note.
  • That shared blind spots are solved. They are discounted and checked, not removed.
  • That this is cheaper. It is slower and spends tens of times the tokens. In the first pilot four mid-class seats plus a frontier hub cost less per item by dollars than twelve samples of the frontier model; in the second, the same panel cost seven times the frontier model's one pass, and the matched condition was not run.
  • That the pilots show the panel is more accurate. They do not. The first showed the panel answered when a single frontier endpoint declined. The second, on harder tasks, found the panel below the frontier model's one pass and below its own best seat, with intervals that include zero, and found the hub's net effect on accuracy zero in one run and negative in the other. Neither preregistered test has been passed.

Products and systems today

The pieces exist separately, and since early 2026 the panel itself is a shipped product. No known system combines tiers, pickerless bidding, a hub that only questions, and a cross-user error catalogue.

Multi-agent debate (research since 2023)
Several model instances answer, read each other's answers, and revise over a fixed number of rounds, with measurable gains on math and factual questions. Falls short: usually one model talking to copies of itself, no tiers, no neutral hub, no memory, and it stops on a round count rather than on agreement.
LLM councils (open source, November 2025)
Several models answer through one API broker, anonymously rank each other's answers, and a chairman model writes the final response. Falls short: the chairman authors, which this design forbids; one round; nothing is remembered.
Council products (2026)
Perplexity's Model Council (February 2026, opened to Pro subscribers in July) sends one prompt to between two and eight models and has a chair synthesize them, flagging where they converge or clash. Council AI runs twenty-plus models from nine labs to a moderator's verdict and keeps a per-user memory; Roundtable runs three models and a moderator. Falls short: in every case a synthesizer authors the answer; there is no critique-and-revise loop that ends on agreement, no release gate, and the only memory is of the user, not of who was wrong.
Mixture-of-agents
Layered aggregation, where each layer's models read the previous layer's outputs and an aggregator produces the answer. Falls short: the layers loosely resemble tiers, but aggregators author, there is no critique dialogue, and there is no memory.
Contract-net bidding (1980)
The ancestor of step 2: tasks are announced, agents bid. Falls short: a manager awards the task. Here nothing awards, and critique does the sorting.
Routers and orchestrators
Pick one model, or assign work from the top. The opposite of this design, on purpose.

The catalogue has no close analog. The nearest things are evaluation harnesses (static test sets) and per-app lessons-learned memories; neither feeds a live critique loop across users.

Known risks

  • Incentives and terms of service. A catalogue that scores each lab's models against the others is a public scorecard, and some labs restrict using their outputs to evaluate or improve competitors. Expect friction, and expect the choice of who supplies the hub to matter.
  • The question budget is a tuning problem. Too small and the hub is a rubber stamp; too large and the Apex tier never ships. At a budget of six, the hub in the second pilot asked 5.1 questions per item, used all six on 79 of 104 tasks, and sent back 92% of right answers.
  • Deference. Even with seats instead of names, models can often recognize each other's style. Anonymized critique reduces caving; it doesn't eliminate it. Kraidia and colleagues study persuasion-driven adversarial influence in multi-agent debate [20] and are the reference for this risk; the designated dissenter and the verifiable-checks rule are the answer to it.
  • Moving targets. Always-latest versions mean behavior shifts without notice. The version stamps in the catalogue are the defense, and they have to be checked, not assumed.

Pilot design

Everything above rests on two claims that can be tested cheaply: that the panel beats the best single model where answers can be checked, and that the hub catches what the panel misses. The pilots are built to be argued with, so the metrics are fixed before the first run.

Pilot 1

Tasks
64 freshly written items across three checkable types: 24 arithmetic and word problems with a numeric answer, 20 data-table questions with a computed answer, and 20 short coding tasks with hidden unit tests. The answers are produced by the same code that writes the prompts, so the key is right by construction, and no public benchmark item is used, so contamination is not in play. Factual lookups that must cite a resolvable source are deferred until web-enabled seats are in place.
Conditions
(a) The best single frontier model, one pass. (b) The same model given the panel's token budget: k samples with a majority vote (or a self-review pick for code), then a self-critique pass, where k is set per task type from the tokens the panel actually spent. (c) The panel with the hub, at the mid tier, running the prototype's six steps prompt for prompt. (d) The panel with the hub removed, to isolate what the hub adds.
Metrics
Accuracy per condition with confidence intervals; tokens and wall-clock per item; the hub's catch rate (answers the panel agreed on, the hub sent back, and that were in fact wrong) and its false-alarm rate; and the deadlock rate.
What would falsify the design
If (c) does not beat (b) on the checkable tasks, the panel is not earning its cost. If (c) does not beat (d), the hub is decoration. Either result is reported as found.
Status
Run on September 5, 2026. Results follow.

Pilot 2

The first pilot's task set was too easy to separate the conditions, so the second was built to be harder and larger, and to measure three things the first could not: what critique does on its own, what the hub does to answers that were already wrong, and what the flag is worth.

Tasks
240 freshly written items in six checkable types, all with keys produced by the code that writes the prompts and none from a public benchmark: 40 multi-step exact computations (longer simulations, four-factor products, four-divisor counts, business-day dates across six holidays); 40 three-part composites and 40 four-part composites, each part an independent problem, where only a fully correct answer counts; 50 data questions over 80-row tables that need a join, with duplicate rows to be counted once; 30 coding tasks with larger hidden test sets; and 40 five-house logic puzzles, each with a unique solution proven by exhaustive search. Factual lookups with a required source are still deferred.
Conditions
(a) The frontier model, Claude Opus 5, one pass at temperature 0.3. Each of the four seats alone, one pass. (c) The panel with the hub, at the mid tier, the prototype's six steps prompt for prompt. (c2) The panel with the hub a second time, where the hub reads the catalogue the first pass built, to test whether the catalogue sharpens its questions. (d) The panel with the hub removed. The compute-matched single-model conditions (b, token-matched, and bd, dollar-matched) were specified but not run; see the readout.
The subset
The frontier model and the four seats ran all 240 tasks. The three panel conditions ran a 104-task subset chosen from the seats' results: every task that at least one seat missed on its own (74) plus 30 that all four seats got right, so that harm done to right answers could be measured. The same 104 tasks ran in c, c2 and d, and every paired comparison below uses the other conditions' results on those same 104.
New measures
Accuracy of the panel's first draft before any critique, so critique's contribution is separated from the hub's; accuracy after critique and before the hub; items with no answer counted separately from wrong answers, with the serving provider logged per call; declines, where a seat or the hub gave up on a step, recorded per task; flags split by the gate that raised them; and the hub's checks, counted and scored.
What would falsify the design
Unchanged: if (c) does not beat (b), the panel is not earning its cost; if (c) does not beat (d), the hub is decoration.
Status
The frontier baseline was run in early September 2026 (committed September 6) and kept. The seats and the three panel conditions ran October 14 to 17, 2026, on a local machine, after a GitHub account suspension cost the September panel runs (the repository README has the account). One answer key was found wrong after the runs and corrected before scoring (see *What the pilot showed about the pilot*).

Readout

  • Test 1, the panel earns its cost against a compute-matched single model: not shown. The point estimate favors the panel by 3.1 points and the interval includes zero.
  • Test 2, the hub adds value: not testable in this run. The panel was right before the hub saw it on all 64 tasks, so the hub's catch rate is undefined. It asked 3.5 questions per item, sent back 56 of 64 right answers, and never turned a right answer wrong.
  • Every answered item was right, in every condition. The single frontier model's misses were not wrong answers. On 8 of the 20 coding prompts (plain tasks: base conversion, a CSV splitter, a range parser) the lab's own endpoint returned an empty, content-filtered reply on every one of seven attempts, at both temperatures. Twelve samples got around it on all but 2 prompts. The panel answered all 64, with a seat from the same lab on it. This is the robustness the bidding rule was designed for, arrived at from the wrong direction: nothing assigns the work, so a seat that declines a component is simply outbid. The second pilot found three more places a participant can drop out after the bid: in a draft or revision, in a vote or critique, and at the hub itself. The decline rule under Release conditions covers them.
  • Deadlocks ran 12.5% with the hub and 14.1% without. Unanimity within three rounds fails about one time in seven even when every seat is right, and at this difficulty the flag carried no information: flagged and unflagged answers were all correct.
  • Cost. By tokens the panel with hub spent 5.6 times the compute-matched single model. By dollars it cost less per item ($0.18 against $0.21), because mid-class tokens are several times cheaper than frontier tokens. Matching compute by tokens was the wrong currency for tiers priced by class; the next version matches by dollars as well.

What the pilot showed about the pilot

  • The task set was too easy. A ceiling at 100% on answered items cannot show a difference between conditions, and cannot give the hub anything to catch. The next set has to be hard enough that the panel misses some.
  • Three harness faults would each have biased the comparison against the single model, and each was found in the dry runs or the first pass and fixed before the numbers above: a token cap that truncated long answers, empty replies counted as answers, and a fallback host that returned filtered replies. Provider routing is now pinned to each lab's own endpoint and recorded per call.
  • Rate limits on a new account distorted wall-clock time, not results: 1,525 retried calls in the compute-matched condition, none in the panel conditions.

The next pilot this version called for (tasks calibrated to 60 to 80% single-model accuracy, at least 200 items, a dollar-matched baseline, a hub-budget sweep, a frontier-tier panel, web-enabled seats, a replication of the refusal finding) was partly run as Pilot 2: the harder and larger task set and the replication on a second task set were done; the matched baselines, the budget sweep, the frontier tier and the web-enabled seats were not.

Readout

  • The result in one sentence. The hub was Claude Opus 5 and the baseline was Claude Opus 5. Writing alone, in one pass, it outscored the panel it was forbidden to write for: 101 of 104 against 96 with the hub and 97 without it. The pilot's question was whether critique and a hub lift four mid-priced seats above the best of them alone; the answer on these tasks is no (96 and 97 against 98), and every one of the nine paired point estimates is against the panel.
  • Test 1, the panel earns its cost against a compute-matched single model: not run, and the plain reading is that it would not have passed. Conditions b and bd were left for after the panel runs, to be paid for only if the panel result made them worth it. A compute-matched frontier condition is given more samples and a self-critique pass on top of the one pass the panel did not beat, so there is no reading on which this panel passes test 1.
  • Test 2, the hub adds value: not passed. The panel without the hub scored one task higher than the panel with it, -1.0 point [-7.7, +5.8]; with the catalogue the gap grew to -3.8 [-11.5, +3.8]. The intervals include zero, so the pilot does not show the hub is decoration either; it shows that at 104 tasks a hub effect, if there is one, is smaller than the pilot can see.
  • Underpowered, and sized by budget. At 104 tasks a paired difference smaller than roughly six to eight points cannot be distinguished from zero, and every between-condition difference the pilot measured is between one and five points. The pilot was sized by what the runs would cost, not by the effect it needed to see. Two runs of the same condition (c and c2) differ on 15 of 104 tasks, and their first drafts, which no critique or hub has touched, already differ on 10; the stage-level noise is of the same order as every effect.
  • The hub is not interrogating; it is adding rounds. It sent back 96 of 104 answers in c and 92 in c2, used all six questions on three quarters of the tasks in c and two thirds in c2, and its catch rate equals its send-back rate: a hub that returns 92% of right answers catches wrong ones by the same motion. It repaired four answers in c, all four of them answers critique had just broken, and none in c2; it broke four each time. Net, zero and minus four. The release conditions did not operate as a stopping rule: with the budget exhausted on most tasks and the panel deadlocked on 40 to 51% of them, half the answers shipped flagged.
  • What the flag is worth. Answers released with all three gates met were right 98.1% of the time in c and 97.6% in c2; flagged answers 86.5% and 84.1%, and the gap passes a one-sided Fisher test at 0.05 in both runs (p = 0.030 and 0.026; two-sided 0.060 and 0.047). But every wrong flagged answer in both runs was a deadlock. The hub's own gate added 10 and 15 flags on answers the panel had agreed on, all of them right, and the check gate added one, right. The information in the flag is the agreement gate's, which the panel without the hub also has; there the deadlock split points the same way and does not reach significance (p = 0.23). The first pilot's flag told the user nothing; this one tells the user that a deadlocked panel is more often wrong, which is a finding about agreement, not about interrogation.
  • The checks were wrong more often than the answers they checked. Seven of the ten failed arithmetic and code checks were on right answers, three of the four releases a check blocked were right, and at most one outcome was changed by a check. The verifiable-checks rule is the design's mechanical safeguard; as run, with the hub writing the expressions, it was a source of false alarms.
  • The catalogue got the weakest possible test, and the comparison is confounded. The catalogue the hub reads is four lines, one per seat: run counts, how often the seat's drafts were corrected, its challenges overruled, its checks failed, and up to three recent challenge snippets. Nothing in it is about the task at hand. The first pass's hub read it too, because the file rebuilds from the first task of the run, so c against c2 is a small digest against a fuller one, not none against some; and the two runs also differ in the harness version (c was run mostly before the decline rule, with most of its tasks re-run after give-ups; c2 entirely after it) and in the random seed that picks the proposer, dissenter and revisers. The second pass scored 2.9 points below the first, [-10.6, +3.8], within the noise. The reading is that the catalogue made no visible difference and that this pilot could not have seen one.
  • Critique on its own was a wash, and the breaks have an author. Across the three runs critique and revision fixed 14 wrong first drafts and broke 16 right ones. DeepSeek's revisions were the panel's net gain (17 fixes against 5 breaks); Sonnet's and Gemini's were net losses (10 against 16, and 6 against 16), GPT's about even (14 against 12). Weighted agreement was not in play at the mid tier (every seat's vote counts the same), and the records show why it would need to be: the weaker seats on these tasks were overturning the strongest seat's right answers.
  • The composites were lost after the draft, not in it. The one task type built for decomposition, bidding and stitching is the type the panel lost most; but of its 20 wrong parts, 15 went wrong in a later revision or hub reply, and four of the other five were repaired and broken again. Decomposition, bidding and stitching did their job; the rounds that followed undid it.
  • The best seat alone matched the frontier model. DeepSeek V4 Pro scored 97.5% on all 240 tasks, 1.2 points above the frontier model's one pass with the interval including zero, and 9 to 11 points above each of the other three seats with every interval excluding zero. See the labelling caveat: it is that lab's top model at a mid-tier price. Note also that the subset is biased in the panel's favour on this comparison: all six of DeepSeek's own misses are in it by construction, so 98 of 104 is a pessimistic reading of a re-run, while the panel got fresh draws. The comparison most favourable to the panel still came out against it.
  • Cost. By dollars the panel with hub cost seven times the frontier model's one pass per task and thirteen times the best seat alone; the panel without the hub, three and a half times the frontier one pass. By tokens the panel with hub spent 59 times the one pass, and by wall clock it took 323 seconds against 16. The first pilot's point that a mid-class panel can be cheaper by dollars than a matched frontier condition has no comparison here, because that condition was not run.
  • The panel answered every task, but that is a cheaper property than it looks. The frontier endpoint declined 6 of the 30 code tasks on every attempt, as it had declined 8 of 20 in the first pilot: a second task set, run within days of the first on the same account. DeepSeek alone returned nothing on 5 of 240. All three panel conditions answered all 104, including the tasks where a seat or the hub gave up partway. But Sonnet, GPT and Gemini alone each answered all 240; the cheap fix for one endpoint's refusals is a second single model at under a cent a task, not a panel.

What the pilot showed about the pilot

  • The task set was still too easy for the frontier model. The target was 50 to 85% single-model accuracy. The frontier model scored 96.2% on all 240 and the four seats 86.7% to 97.5%. The subset mechanism concentrated the tasks at least one seat missed, but the hub still had only 8 and 7 wrong answers to work with, which is why its repair and harm rates rest on single digits.
  • Run-to-run noise is as large as every difference between conditions. The first and second panel-with-hub runs differ on 15 of 104 tasks (9 right to wrong, 6 wrong to right); the panel with and without the hub on 13; the second pass and the panel without the hub on 16. Their first drafts differ on 10, 8 and 12, and most draft-stage differences do not survive to the final answer: each stage adds its own noise rather than passing the draft's through. Every between-condition difference is between one and five points, so none can be separated from the noise at 104 tasks. A pilot that wants to see a three-point effect needs several times the tasks, or the same condition run more than once so the noise is measured rather than assumed.
  • One answer key was wrong. The generator for one kind of math part charged a whole tax bracket even when the income fell below its top. Of the 240 tasks only one part of one three-part composite was affected; every condition, single-model and panel, gave the correct figure and was scored wrong. It was found by scoring the composites part by part after the runs, the key was corrected, the generator fixed (regenerating the task set with the fix reproduces the corrected file exactly), and the eight stored records rescored; the correction moved a single task in every condition and no paired difference between the panel and the single models. The scan that found it (every non-code part on which five or more conditions were scored wrong on the same value) found no other case.
  • The harness accepted malformed replacements. A revision, or a corrected answer sent with a reply to the hub, replaced the panel's answer unchecked. Six of the panel's 26 wrong answers are right values that had lost the request's FINAL: line this way, two of them counted as hub harm. The single-model conditions, which never rewrite, lost one answer of 93 to the same rule. The harness now refuses a replacement that has lost the format the standing answer had, keeps the standing text, and records the refusal; the records in this pilot predate the rule and are reported as scored, with the six listed.
  • Two more budget faults were found, both counting against the panel, and a third place a participant can drop out. First, in the priced trial: DeepSeek V4 Pro accepts a low reasoning-effort setting but does not bound its thinking by it, so on the JSON-only steps it spent the whole 5,000-token cap thinking and returned nothing on 62 of 133 calls, and six of the trial's twelve tasks died after six retries each; the other four models had no call errors. Thinking is now turned off for that model on those steps only; answer-writing steps keep the seat's normal reasoning. The trial's results were cleared and condition c started fresh. Second, in the full run of c: six of 104 tasks ended in an error because one model gave up after its retries and the harness ended the task, although the paper says the panel routes around a seat that declines. Four were DeepSeek calls at the 16,000-token answer cap that returned nothing six times (three replies to the hub and one revision); one was a content-filter refusal by the hub; one was the hub overrunning its 5,000-token cap. The fix is the decline rule, described under *Lines elsewhere in the paper*, and the six tasks were retried under it; tasks completed before the fix were unchanged, since the new code runs only when a call gives up. Third: the hub's 5,000-token cap, with no reasoning budget, was overrun on 72 attempts across the two hub runs. It is a setting that touched the hub conditions only, and it should be fixed before another dollar is spent on them.
  • Most of condition c's tasks ran more than once. The calls log shows that the first full run of c stalled for about seven hours when the machine slept and then lost the network, which ended about 75 tasks at their first call; roughly 78 of the 104 kept c records are a second or third run of their task (c2 and d ran every task once). The re-runs were not conditioned on correctness, and the records keep the last run; but "the second run reads the catalogue the first built" sits beside the fact that the first run was itself mostly re-runs.
  • A seat that returns nothing can be a budget setting, not a weak model. This is the third time the pilot has found it: a 1,500-token JSON cap in September that Sonnet 5 and DeepSeek V4 spent entirely on thinking and collapsed the panel on 89 of 108 tasks; the DeepSeek reasoning setting above; and the answer cap on long replies. The per-step budgets are part of the design, not a harness detail, and the paper should say so. The same holds for the settings that shape the single-model results: September's harness, under the earlier caps, had DeepSeek alone at 92.9% and a 108-task subset (those seat runs were lost with the Codespace; the figures survive only in the author's notes); October's, with the budgets fixed, has 97.5% and 104. That is a four-point move from settings alone, larger than any difference between conditions in this pilot.
  • The tiers were matched by price, not by class. The paper defines the mid tier as "Sonnet-class and equivalents". The pilot filled DeepSeek's seat with that lab's top model because it is priced at the mid tier, and that seat turned out to be the strongest on the panel and the equal of the frontier baseline. The pilot therefore does not measure a panel of four mid-class models, and v1.3 should say the seats were matched by price band and let the reader weigh the DeepSeek result accordingly. (Google's position moved during the pilot: Gemini 3.8 Flash was its newest model when the seats were chosen, and the frontier Gemini 4 Argon arrived days before the seat runs; the price-band framing holds either way.)
  • The baseline and the panel ran six weeks apart. The frontier baseline is the September run, kept because its code path (one call, no JSON steps, exempt from the reasoning budget) was untouched by the fixes, every call finished normally, and none of its misses were truncations. The seats and the panel ran in October on a local machine, after a GitHub account suspension deleted the Codespace that held the September panel runs. Model versions were pinned by id throughout, but an endpoint's behavior can change without notice, and the paper's own moving-targets risk applies to its pilot.

What this pilot does and does not show

It shows:

  • Four mid-priced seats with critique and a frontier hub did not beat one pass of the frontier model on 104 tasks weighted toward the ones the seats miss, and did not beat the best of the four seats alone. The point estimates are 4.8 and 1.9 points against the panel with the intervals including zero; the second hub pass sits 7.7 points below the frontier model with an interval that excludes zero.
  • Removing the hub did not lower accuracy: the panel without it scored one task higher. The hub, as configured, caught wrong answers by sending back nearly everything, repaired 4 of 15 wrong answers across two runs (all four of them answers critique had just broken), broke 8 right ones (two by a lost format line), and changed the count of right answers by 0 and then -4.
  • The flag separates wrong answers from right ones at p = 0.03 in both hub runs, and the whole of that information is the agreement gate's: every wrong flagged answer was a deadlock, and the hub's own gate flagged 25 right answers and no wrong one.
  • The hub's checks failed on right answers seven times out of ten and changed at most one outcome.
  • A second pass with the catalogue did not improve on the first, and the pilot could not have seen it if it had.
  • Critique and revision across three runs fixed 14 wrong first drafts and broke 16 right ones; the gains came mostly from one seat's revisions and the losses mostly from two others'.
  • The composites were lost in the rounds after the draft, not in decomposition, bidding or stitching.
  • Run-to-run variation between two runs of the same condition was 15 of 104 tasks, the size of every difference the pilot measured.
  • The panel with hub cost seven times the frontier one pass per task, with no accuracy gain to set against it.
  • The panel answered every task, including six code tasks the frontier endpoint refused and five tasks the best seat returned nothing on, which repeats the first pilot's finding on a second task set; so did three of the four seats alone.

It does not show:

  • That the hub is decoration. The c minus d interval runs from -7.7 to +5.8 points, and the hub saw eight wrong answers.
  • That the panel with the hub is less accurate than the frontier model. That interval includes zero, though the point estimate and both hub runs sit on that side, and the second run's interval does not.
  • Anything about test 1, since the compute-matched conditions were not run.
  • Anything about a frontier-tier panel, other hub budgets or round budgets, weighted agreement, open-ended tasks, or factual lookups.
  • Whether DeepSeek's result reflects a mid-tier model, since it is not one.

The six contributions against the records

The paper claims six contributions. What the records of this pilot say about each:

  1. A hub that never writes. Run as a frontier model interrogating a mid-priced panel, the hub's net effect on right answers was zero and then minus four, and the same model writing alone outscored the panel it interrogated. The design's bet that questions beat a draft did not hold on checkable tasks at this tier.
  2. Bidding, not routing. Bids were placed and every component was drafted by a bidder; no seat declined a draft in the kept runs. The first assembled drafts were right on 100, 96 and 96 of 104, as good as the best seat alone on the same tasks. Bidding did its job; nothing in the pilot tests it against routing.
  3. Seat-anonymous, evidence-required critique with a designated dissenter. Critique fixed 14 and broke 16. The evidence requirement did not stop revisers from accepting wrong challenges (several of the twenty breaks follow a challenge whose figure was itself wrong), and seat anonymity did not stop the weaker seats from overturning the stronger one.
  4. Release conditions instead of a round count. The conditions did not operate as a stopping rule: the hub used its whole budget on most tasks, the panel deadlocked on 40 to 51%, and half the answers shipped flagged. The verifiable-checks part of the rule produced more false alarms than catches.
  5. The failure catalogue. Given the weakest possible test: a four-line digest of per-seat counts, read by both hub runs, with no task-level content. No effect was visible and none could have been.
  6. Cross-lab tiers with weighted agreement. The tiers were matched by price, not class, and weighted agreement was not in play at a single tier. The records give the first evidence that it would matter: the strongest seat's right answers were overturned by the others' revisions.

Next pilot

In this order. First, what is free: the stage-by-stage analysis in proposal/v1.3-analysis-notes.md has been done on these records and belongs in the paper before any new run. Second, the hub's budget and the format guard: give the hub a reasoning budget or a larger reply cap, and keep the rule that refuses a revision or correction that drops the answer format; both touched the panel conditions only, and a rerun under them is not comparable with these records on the affected tasks without saying so. Third, measure the noise directly: run condition c twice under identical settings on the same 104 tasks before any new condition, about $45. Fourth, test what the records point at: a hub budget below six, so the hub asks rather than exhausts; a check rule that only blocks release when the check's expression is itself verified; and a reviser who must show the figure a challenge disputes before changing it. Fifth, the compute-matched conditions b and bd, about $50 on the same 104, which would confirm what the one pass already shows and are the lowest priority for that reason. A frontier-tier panel needs an argument first: critique and a hub did not help four seats one class below the hub, so the paper has to say why they would help seats of the hub's own class, and what result would falsify that, before the $150 to $500 it would cost. Label every seat by price band and by position in its lab's line-up, and pick DeepSeek's mid-class model if the mid tier is what is being measured. Replicate the refusal finding on a second account and on a later date; both pilots' baselines ran in the same week.
